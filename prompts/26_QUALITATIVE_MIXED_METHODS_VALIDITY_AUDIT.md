# RIGOR — QUALITATIVE AND MIXED-METHODS VALIDITY AUDIT

## 0. Objective

Audit whether the qualitative or mixed-methods evidence actually supports the claims made by a social-science manuscript.

This module is intentionally method-pluralist. It must evaluate the work against:
1. the paper's stated epistemic and methodological commitments;
2. the inferential burden of the claims actually made;
3. accepted standards relevant to that design.

Do **not** force positivist, interpretivist, ethnographic, process-tracing, comparative-historical, case-study, interview, focus-group, archival, discourse, content-analysis, or mixed-methods work into a single template.

When the RIGOR master workflow is active, the mandatory Submission Readiness Gate and Journal Targeting Research remain upstream of this module.

---

## 1. Closed vs open behavior

### CLOSED / NEAR-FINAL
- Audit validity, consistency, evidentiary support, and implementation of the claimed method.
- Report actual problems only.
- Do not demand a different philosophical framework or redesign simply because another design is possible.
- Remedies should preserve the substantive claim when a reasonable correct fix exists.

### OPEN / IN DEVELOPMENT
- In addition to auditing, recommend the **minimum adequate** design, evidence, coding, triangulation, or integration work needed to resolve a material weakness.
- Apply the RIGOR cost-benefit gate.
- Do not emit a wish list.
- Escalate to new data collection only if lower-cost remedies cannot adequately resolve the claim at risk.

---

## 2. First reconstruct the design

Before criticizing anything, state:

- research question;
- type of qualitative/mixed-methods design;
- unit(s) of analysis;
- case(s);
- population or universe to which claims refer;
- data sources;
- sampling/case-selection logic;
- data collection process;
- coding/interpretive process;
- temporal scope;
- comparison structure;
- causal/descriptive/interpretive/theory-building objective;
- role of the qualitative component in a mixed-methods design;
- intended inferential scope.

If any of these cannot be reconstructed, mark them **UNVERIFIABLE/UNDERSPECIFIED** rather than guessing.

---

## 3. Claim–evidence map

For every central claim, classify it as primarily:

- descriptive;
- interpretive;
- mechanism/process;
- causal;
- comparative;
- typological;
- theory-building;
- scope/transferability;
- mixed-methods integration.

Then map:

\[
\text{Claim}
\rightarrow
\text{Evidence}
\rightarrow
\text{Selection}
\rightarrow
\text{Analytic procedure}
\rightarrow
\text{Inferential warrant}
\]

A claim fails this module when the chain breaks materially.

---

## 4. Mandatory qualitative audit blocks

Run every block that applies.

### A. Research-question / design fit

Check whether the stated qualitative design can answer the question posed.

Examples:
- exploratory interviews supporting an exploratory claim can be adequate;
- the same interviews may be inadequate for a population prevalence claim;
- process tracing can support within-case causal-process claims but does not automatically identify an average treatment effect;
- discourse analysis can support claims about framing/meaning but not automatically claims about behavioral prevalence.

Do not criticize a design for failing to answer a question the paper never asks.

### B. Case selection

Audit:
- why these cases;
- selection on outcome;
- selection on treatment/exposure;
- typical/deviant/most-likely/least-likely logic;
- comparative-case logic;
- temporal case boundaries;
- whether case selection is consistent with the claimed inference;
- whether negative or disconfirming cases are structurally excluded;
- whether the manuscript generalizes beyond what the selection supports.

Distinguish strategic case selection from representative sampling.

### C. Participant / source sampling

When applicable check:
- sampling frame;
- recruitment;
- purposive/theoretical/snowball/convenience sampling;
- eligibility;
- nonresponse/refusal;
- gatekeeper effects;
- missing relevant actor groups;
- stopping rule;
- whether saturation is claimed and, if so, what kind and how established;
- whether the paper makes prevalence/population claims unsupported by the sampling design.

Do not require "saturation" if the stated method does not use it as a validity criterion.

### D. Data provenance and chain of evidence

Check:
- source identity/type;
- date/time relevance;
- archival provenance;
- interview timing relative to events;
- first-hand vs second-hand evidence;
- transcription;
- translation;
- document selection;
- source duplication;
- quote provenance;
- whether central claims can be traced to identifiable evidence;
- whether evidence could have been selected after knowing the desired conclusion.

Confidentiality may legitimately restrict disclosure. Do not equate anonymization with unverifiability.

### E. Coding and analytic procedure

If coding is used, reconstruct:
- unit of coding;
- codebook;
- deductive vs inductive codes;
- code creation/revision;
- coder assignment;
- adjudication;
- software if relevant;
- aggregation from codes to themes/constructs;
- treatment of overlapping codes;
- treatment of missing/uncodable material.

Check for:
- code definitions that drift;
- circular coding that embeds the conclusion;
- examples inconsistent with code definitions;
- theme labels unsupported by coded evidence;
- counts derived from coding that do not match the coding universe.

### F. Inter-coder reliability / agreement

Only require or audit formal inter-coder reliability when it is appropriate to the paper's stated analytic framework or when the paper itself makes reliability claims.

If reported:
- verify statistic used;
- number of coders;
- unit of analysis;
- chance correction if claimed;
- training vs evaluation sample;
- whether agreement was measured before or after adjudication;
- whether reliability claims generalize beyond the checked subset.

Never impose Cohen's kappa, Krippendorff's alpha, or similar statistics mechanically on interpretive designs for which consensus/reflexive coding is the intended method.

### G. Triangulation and source independence

Check:
- whether multiple sources are genuinely independent;
- whether "triangulation" is only repetition of the same underlying source;
- whether sources converge;
- whether contradictions are reported;
- whether different source types answer different parts of the claim;
- whether one weak source is being rhetorically multiplied into several pieces of evidence.

### H. Negative cases and disconfirming evidence

Check whether the analysis:
- searches for contrary evidence when the method implies it should;
- reports negative cases;
- explains exceptions;
- modifies propositions when disconfirming cases require it;
- cherry-picks only confirming quotations/cases.

Do not demand exhaustive negative-case analysis when the claim is explicitly descriptive and narrow.

### I. Quotations and textual evidence

Check:
- quotes actually support the accompanying interpretation;
- ellipses do not reverse or materially change meaning;
- quote attribution is consistent;
- translated quotations preserve the relevant content where verification is possible;
- isolated quotes are not used to claim prevalence;
- repeated quotes are not treated as independent observations;
- excerpts are not inconsistent with the stated coding/theme.

### J. Process tracing / causal mechanism evidence

If the paper makes within-case causal or mechanism claims, audit:
- hypothesized sequence;
- temporal ordering;
- alternative explanations;
- diagnostic evidence;
- expected observations under competing explanations;
- hoop/smoking-gun/doubly-decisive claims if such terminology is used;
- whether each mechanistic step has evidence;
- whether absence of evidence is incorrectly treated as evidence of absence;
- whether post-outcome narratives are mistaken for ex ante causal evidence.

Do not import process-tracing terminology into papers that do not use a mechanism design unless it clarifies an actual inferential flaw.

### K. Comparative qualitative inference

For comparative case designs check:
- common vs differing dimensions;
- temporal comparability;
- measurement comparability;
- case-specific confounders;
- whether the claimed contrast exists;
- whether the comparative logic matches the case-selection logic;
- whether one case is carrying the entire conclusion;
- whether the manuscript distinguishes necessary/sufficient/configurational claims correctly.

### L. Reflexivity / positionality

Audit only to the extent relevant to the stated method and claims.

Check whether:
- researcher role affects access, elicitation, interpretation, or observed behavior;
- the paper makes claims of neutrality/objectivity inconsistent with the collection process;
- positionality is invoked rhetorically without explaining analytic consequences;
- reflexive practices claimed in methods are actually implemented.

Do not require a positionality section merely as a ritual if it has no bearing on the design or target journal.

### M. Ethics and consent as validity-relevant issues

This is not a legal/IRB compliance audit.

Only flag ethics/consent issues when they affect:
- data provenance;
- selection;
- response behavior;
- source credibility;
- reproducibility of the claimed procedure;
- interpretation.

Do not speculate about misconduct.

## 5. Mixed-methods integration audit

For mixed-methods papers, identify the design:
- convergent;
- explanatory sequential;
- exploratory sequential;
- embedded;
- multiphase;
- other explicitly stated design.

Then audit:

### A. Role clarity
What does each component contribute that the other does not?

### B. Connection
Are samples, cases, variables, phases, or constructs actually connected?

### C. Integration point
Where are the strands integrated?
- design;
- sampling;
- analysis;
- interpretation;
- joint display;
- theory development.

### D. Convergence and divergence
Does the paper report:
- corroboration;
- complementarity;
- contradiction;
- silence/no evidence?

A mixed-methods paper must not report only convergent evidence if the underlying strands materially disagree.

### E. Construct alignment
Do quantitative variables and qualitative concepts actually measure the same construct when the paper treats them as equivalent?

### F. Inferential hierarchy
Does one strand provide the central inference while the other is merely illustrative? If so, the paper should not portray them as equally identifying/supporting the claim.

### G. Sequencing
If one phase informs another, verify that chronology permits that influence.

## 6. Qualitative causal-claim discipline

For any causal wording, ask:

1. What exact causal claim is being made?
2. What rival explanation would produce the same observed narrative?
3. What evidence discriminates among them?
4. Is temporal ordering established?
5. Is the evidence generated after outcomes were known?
6. Does case selection condition on the outcome?
7. Are mechanism claims confused with effect identification?
8. Is absence of a rival explanation treated as proof of the preferred one?

Classify the claim:
- supported;
- consistent with evidence but not discriminating;
- weakly tested;
- not tested;
- contradicted;
- not identified by the supplied qualitative design.

---

## 7. Qualitative quantification

If the paper reports counts, percentages, frequencies, code prevalence, or "most/many/few" statements:

- identify denominator;
- check whether sampling supports the interpretation;
- distinguish descriptive count within the corpus from population prevalence;
- verify counts across tables/text;
- check whether multiple coded segments per participant are being mistaken for participant counts;
- check whether one participant can contribute to multiple categories;
- check missing/uncodable units.

Route numerical inconsistencies to the Statistical Consistency module as linked findings when appropriate.

---

## 8. Severity

### FATAL
The central claim cannot be supported by the qualitative/mixed-methods design as implemented, and preserving it requires a fundamentally different evidentiary basis.

### MAJOR
A material break in case selection, evidence chain, coding, mechanism inference, or mixed-methods integration threatens a central claim.

### MODERATE
A real validity problem affects a secondary claim or materially weakens credibility without overturning the main conclusion.

### MINOR
A localized reporting, documentation, coding-description, quotation, or integration issue that does not materially change the substantive inference.

Use:
- **VERIFIED**
- **INTERNALLY CONFIRMED**
- **LIKELY**
- **UNVERIFIABLE**

Never fabricate unavailable interview transcripts, codebooks, archives, field notes, sampling frames, or coding outputs.

## 11. Anti-watering-down rule

Before narrowing a substantive claim, ask whether it can be preserved by:
- clarifying the case universe;
- restoring missing evidentiary support already present;
- correcting coding/integration;
- adding a feasible negative-case check;
- making the causal mechanism test discriminating;
- explicitly linking existing evidence;
- fixing a mischaracterized sampling claim.

Only recommend claim narrowing when preserving the claim requires unsupported evidence, unavailable data, a fundamentally different design, or disproportionate work.

If scope is narrowed, record the lost scope explicitly.

---

## 12. Implementation completeness

A qualitative remedy is not implementation-ready until the report identifies, where applicable:

- methods paragraph;
- sampling description;
- case-selection rationale;
- coding section;
- affected theme/claim;
- quotes/examples;
- tables/joint displays;
- abstract;
- introduction;
- discussion;
- conclusion;
- appendix/supplement.

If a central concept is redefined, perform a manuscript-wide impact sweep for every location where that concept drives interpretation.

---

## 13. Closure standard

A clean audit requires:

1. all applicable design blocks covered;
2. no FATAL/MAJOR findings open;
3. central claim–evidence chains verified;
4. mixed-methods integration verified where applicable;
5. all material closed findings pass downstream/regression verification;
6. two distinct clean passes reveal no new material validity issue.

Otherwise state:

**QUALITATIVE / MIXED-METHODS AUDIT INCOMPLETE**

and specify the missing evidence or unresolved issue.

---

## 14. Final output

Produce:

1. Qualitative/mixed-methods validity verdict.
2. Reconstructed design.
3. Claim–evidence map.
4. Priority findings.
5. Complete findings by root cause.
6. Unverifiable evidence/materials.
7. Mixed-methods integration verdict, if applicable.
8. Minimum adequate work program, OPEN only.
9. Implementation-ready change requirements.
10. Closure status.

Do not impose a different research tradition merely because you prefer it. Audit the paper the authors actually wrote and the claims they actually make.
